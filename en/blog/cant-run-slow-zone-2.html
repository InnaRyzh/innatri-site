"I Physically Can't Run Slow": What to Do When Your Heart Rate Explodes on Easy Runs

Inna Ryzhykh • 2026-07-21 • 8 MIN READ

"Just slow down" is the most useless advice a new runner ever gets.

You're already barely moving. Any slower is walking. And your heart rate is still 170. The watch glows red, the app says zone 4, the internet warns you're doing it all wrong… And a runner who a month ago simply enjoyed their runs is now googling "high heart rate easy run" at midnight, half-convinced something is wrong with their heart.

Sound familiar? Breathe out. Let me walk you through what's actually happening. Spoiler: 9 times out of 10, your heart is fine. Your expectations are what's broken.

Why everyone is obsessed with zone 2 in the first place

Quick foundation. Sports physiologist Stephen Seiler analyzed how elite endurance athletes actually train and found a stubborn pattern: roughly 80% of their training time at low intensity, only 20% at high. His well-known 2010 paper in the International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance laid this out.

80%
of training time - slow and easy
20%
of training time - fast and hard

And it's not just an elite thing. A study in the Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research (2007) split recreational runners into two groups: one ran more of its mileage easy, the other ran more in the moderate, comfortably-hard middle. Months later, the easy-running group had improved its race time noticeably more. Slow running builds the aerobic base: the heart, the capillaries, the mitochondria. Speed gets built on top of that foundation, never instead of it.

So the idea is right. The problem isn't the idea. The problem is how it gets dumped on beginners.

Why you can't do it: the 4 real reasons

1. Your zones are probably calculated wrong. Out of the box, your watch estimates max heart rate as "220 minus age." That formula can miss by 10–12 beats in either direction. Which means the "zone 4" on your screen may be a physiologically normal zone 2–3. Before you panic, recalculate your zones from real data, not a formula. I show how step by step in Running by Heart Rate.

2. Your aerobic base doesn't exist yet. This is the big one to accept. A trained runner has a wide range of speeds between "walking" and "running easy." A beginner doesn't have that range yet: the body hasn't learned to produce energy aerobically while running, so ALL running sits near the ceiling. That's not a defect. That's a starting point. Build the base and the range appears.

3. Cardiac drift. Even at a perfect pace, your heart rate creeps up over 40–60 minutes: you heat up, you lose fluid, and the heart needs more beats for the same work. Starting in zone 2 and finishing in zone 3 is physiology, not failure. Heat and a bad night's sleep each add another 5–10 beats for free.

4. You run slow, but you won't let yourself walk. Walk breaks somehow count as shameful: "I'm a runner, not a walker." Meanwhile run-walk intervals are a standard training tool; people finish marathons on them. A minute of walking when your heart rate has crept up isn't quitting. It's load management.

What to do: the no-panic plan

  • 1. Recalculate your zones from a real max or a field test, not "220 minus age."
  • 2. On easy runs, switch instruments: use the talk test instead of the watch. Full sentences, comfortably? That's an easy run, whatever number is on your wrist. Gasping out fragments? Ease off.
  • 3. Give yourself permission to walk. A "4 minutes run / 1 minute walk" pattern keeps average heart rate down and banks the aerobic minutes this whole thing is about.
  • 4. Give it time. The first real shift shows up after 6–8 weeks of consistency. In six months you won't recognize your runs: same heart rate, different speed. This is woodpecker work: small taps, every week, no heroics.
  • 5. Stop treating slow runs as weak runs. They are the exact thing that makes you fast.

I've lived this as an athlete and with every beginner I've coached. Not one of them could "run in zone 2" in their first month. Every one who didn't quit eventually could. The only variable was patience.

FAQ

Is a high heart rate on easy runs dangerous?
In a healthy person, a high heart rate under load is just the heart doing its job. What deserves attention: chest pain or pressure, dizziness, irregular rhythm, breathlessness out of proportion to the effort. If you have those, see a cardiologist instead of diagnosing yourself by watch. If you don't, build your base in peace.
How long until my running heart rate comes down?
First changes usually show after 6–8 weeks of regular easy running. Meaningful ones take 4–6 months. There is no fast version.
Can I just ignore heart rate completely?
On easy runs, often yes: the talk test beats badly calibrated zones. A heart rate monitor earns its place later, for tracking progress and on long runs.
If I walk more than I run, does it still count?
It's endurance training, and it works. The running share grows on its own. Nobody at a half marathon finish line asks how many minutes you walked in March.

The short version: slow running isn't something you're failing at. It's something you haven't built yet. Huge difference: one sounds like a verdict, the other sounds like a plan.

You can't buy an aerobic base. You can only run it into existence, a little at a time, week after week.
